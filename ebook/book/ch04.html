The $10-per-week version

On a tight budget, buy one or two extra shelf-stable items per regular trip — a can of beans here, a pouch of tuna there — and add one gallon jug of water monthly. Rotation (Chapter 9) turns that trickle into a real supply within a season. If you receive SNAP/EBT or WIC benefits, ask your local office how disaster replacement benefits work in your state, and prioritize shelf-stable proteins and produce (canned) when the budget is thin.

Freeze-dried buckets versus groceries: the honest math

Commercial freeze-dried and dehydrated meals have genuine strengths: very long stated shelf lives (often measured in years to decades, depending on brand and packaging), zero planning at cooking time, and portability. They are also usually several times more expensive per serving than the grocery staples that replace them, their flavors can be unfamiliar to children, and sodium levels vary widely by brand — check labels like any other food.

The grocery pantry's strengths are the mirror image: pennies per serving for rice, oats, beans and pasta; flavors your household already likes; and rotation that refunds its own cost as you eat what you store. Its weaknesses: you must supply the water, fuel and know-how, and shelf lives of everyday packaging are measured in months and years, not decades.

The practical answer for most households is a hybrid: grocery staples as the foundation, plus a small amount of commercial product where it genuinely earns its price — a go-bag meal, an evacuation-ready entrée, a backup for households with no cooking option at all. Never attack-or-buy in panic; test one item of any brand before committing money, and read the nutrition and sodium figures before stocking cases.

Taste-test before you stock

Buy one unit of anything new, cook it on an ordinary night, and score it on the taste-test sheet in the back matter. If the household rates it below four out of five, it does not earn shelf space — no matter the bargain. This one habit prevents the most expensive mistake in preparedness: food nobody eats.
