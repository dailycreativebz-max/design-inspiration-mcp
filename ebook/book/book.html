Emergency Safety Notice

Carbon monoxide kills quietly

Never run a generator, charcoal grill, camp stove, propane heater or any fuel-burning device indoors, in a garage, or in any enclosed or partially enclosed space unless it is specifically approved for that environment and used exactly as directed. Run generators outdoors at least 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, with exhaust pointed away. Keep a battery-powered or battery-backup carbon monoxide alarm working whenever a generator is in use. Symptoms — headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, confusion — mean get into fresh air immediately and call 911.

The five numbers that protect your food
4 hoursHow long a closed refrigerator keeps food safely cold (USDA). After that, discard refrigerated perishables.
48 / 24 hoursHow long a closed full / half-full freezer keeps food safely frozen (USDA).
40 °FThe cold line. Perishables held above 40 °F too long are discarded — never taste to check.
140 °FThe hot line. Keep hot food at or above 140 °F; the 40–140 °F range is the bacterial Danger Zone.
2 hoursMaximum time perishable food sits out — 1 hour when the air is above 90 °F (USDA).
Water rules

When authorities issue a boil-water advisory, boil water at a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 feet) or disinfect per the EPA table in Chapter 3. During a flood, assume floodwater is contaminated: discard any food it touched unless it is in a sealed waterproof can, and follow the salvage steps in Chapter 9. Never ration drinking water — drink what you need today and find more for tomorrow (FEMA).

The gap nobody talks about

American households face a quiet contradiction when they look into emergency food. On one side are generic prepper lists: fifty items, bulk buckets, grain barrels — plenty of telling people what to buy, almost no guidance on what to actually do with it when the power is out, the kids are hungry and the stove is cold. On the other side are commercial freeze-dried buckets: convenient and long-lasting, but often expensive per calorie, unfamiliar to picky eaters, and sometimes heavy on sodium.

Between those two poles sits the reality of most U.S. households: a normal grocery budget, a normal pantry, and normal disasters — a storm that knocks the power out for two days, an ice storm that closes the roads for a week, a hurricane evacuation, a supply hiccup at the store, a paycheck that arrives late. FEMA and Ready.gov encourage every household to keep at least several days of shelf-stable food and water, and to plan toward two weeks where practical.

This book lives in that gap. Its promise is simple: help an ordinary household build an affordable emergency food supply from grocery-store items — the same brands on your shelf today — and turn those items into real meals during power outages, hurricanes, winter storms, wildfires, tornadoes, earthquakes and short-term supply disruptions. No apocalypse talk. No miracle foods. Just planning.

What "prepared" actually means here

Preparedness in this book means four concrete abilities. You can: (1) feed everyone in your household for at least 72 hours with zero cooking, zero electricity and minimal water; (2) keep feeding them for up to 30 days using shelf-stable meals you have tasted and approved; (3) keep the food fresh through rotation instead of letting it expire untouched; and (4) do all of that on a budget that fits inside a normal grocery run.

Notice what is missing from that definition: guarantees. Nobody can guarantee outcomes in a disaster. What you can control is readiness — and readiness built from food you already eat is cheaper, tastier and far more likely to actually get used.

Three layers, one rule

Build the pantry in layers, from foods that need nothing to foods that need time and fuel. The single rule: store what you eat; eat what you store. Every item in every layer should be something your household would happily eat on an ordinary Tuesday.

LayerCoversWhat it holdsNeeds
Layer 1 — ReadyHours 0–72No-cook meals, ready-to-eat protein, crackers, nut butters, shelf milk, fruit cups, jerky, granola, waterNothing — no heat, no water beyond drinking
Layer 2 — TransitionDays 2–14Hot-water meals (couscous, instant rice, oats, ramen), one-pot canned meals, sauces, seasonings, comfort foodsHot water or one burner, modest fuel

Why layers matter

Most outages end before Layer 3 is ever touched — but they always start in Layer 1. A household with deep storage and no ready layer eats cold beans with no can opener; a household with only snack bars runs out of dinner by day two. Layers fix both failure modes.

Water, protein, calories, sanitation — in that order

When money or shelf space is tight, priority runs in this order: water first (you can go weeks without a shopping cart, but only days without water), then ready-to-eat protein (it requires no cooking when the stove is dead), then calories from familiar carbohydrates, then a way to heat water, and finally sanitation supplies — hand sanitizer, disposable gloves, trash bags, paper plates — because an emergency kitchen is also a hygiene problem.

FEMA's baseline is one gallon of water per person per day for drinking and sanitation, with at least a several-day supply on hand and two weeks as a practical goal. Chapter 3 turns that number into a household-specific plan.

The budget truth

You do not need a dedicated "emergency budget." You need a grocery budget with a rotation habit: every week, one or two extra shelf-stable items go in; every week, one pantry meal comes out. Chapter 4 shows the math.

Quantities, Calories and Water

How much food is enough, how much water it takes, and how to plan for the people actually living in your house.

The Emergency Food Playbook · Food Opsec 6
The Emergency Food PlaybookChapter 3

There is no single "correct" number

Emergency-planning sites often publish one universal calorie figure. That is a shortcut, not a fact. Energy needs vary with age, sex, body size, activity, climate and health. The USDA's Dietary Guidelines put typical adult needs in ranges — roughly 1,600–2,400 calories per day for women and 2,000–3,000 for men — and children's needs anywhere from about 1,000 to 3,200 depending on age and activity. Pregnancy and breastfeeding raise needs further; a clinician is the right source for those plans.

Use calories and protein as planning aids, then verify against the labels of the products you actually buy. A sensible planning posture: target roughly 1,800–2,200 calories and 50–60 g of protein per adult per day in your menu math, scale children down by appetite, and adjust upward for hard physical work or cold weather. The FDA Daily Values are a handy yardstick on labels: 2,300 mg sodium, 50 g protein, 28 g fiber and 50 g added sugar each represent a full day's benchmark on a 2,000-calorie reference diet.

Water is the real emergency food

FEMA's planning baseline is one gallon per person per day — about half a gallon for drinking and half for food preparation and sanitation. That is a floor, not a ceiling: hot weather, physical work, pregnancy, illness and children's activity all push needs up. Ready.gov recommends storing at least a several-day supply and working toward two weeks per person; store more if space and budget allow. And one FEMA rule deserves bold type: never ration drinking water — drink what you need today, then find more for tomorrow.

Budget water in four columns: drinking, cooking (grains and pasta drink water you can see; check each recipe's spec strip), sanitation (handwashing, dish cleanup, hygiene) and pets — dogs and cats need their own supply, roughly one ounce of water per pound of body weight per day as a rule of thumb, more in heat.

What to store

The simplest, most reliable supply is commercially bottled water in its original sealed containers — buy it, date it, rotate it, and follow the date on the bottle. For self-stored tap water use food-grade containers (PETE plastic jugs with tight caps are common); thoroughly washed and sanitized first. Never reuse containers that held milk, juice or chemicals — milk residues foster bacteria and chemical residues do not fully rinse out. Store water in a cool, dark place; light and heat shorten its life. Rotate self-stored water about every six months; unopened commercial bottles follow their printed dates.

If storage space is short, remember other sources exist — but they need treatment: the hot-water tank (switch off its power or gas first and close the inlet valve), and ice frozen in advance in jugs and bags, which doubles as cooler fuel. Do not count on the tap: pressure can fail without warning.

Floodwater is contaminated water

After flooding, assume water touching your home is contaminated with sewage, fuel or chemicals. Do not use it for drinking, cooking or washing dishes without treatment — and note that boiling does not remove chemical contaminants: if authorities issue a "do not drink" advisory, only sealed bottled water is safe. Discard any food touched by floodwater (salvage rules for sealed cans are in Chapter 9). Keep children and pets away from standing floodwater.

The $10-per-week version

On a tight budget, buy one or two extra shelf-stable items per regular trip — a can of beans here, a pouch of tuna there — and add one gallon jug of water monthly. Rotation (Chapter 9) turns that trickle into a real supply within a season. If you receive SNAP/EBT or WIC benefits, ask your local office how disaster replacement benefits work in your state, and prioritize shelf-stable proteins and produce (canned) when the budget is thin.

Freeze-dried buckets versus groceries: the honest math

Commercial freeze-dried and dehydrated meals have genuine strengths: very long stated shelf lives (often measured in years to decades, depending on brand and packaging), zero planning at cooking time, and portability. They are also usually several times more expensive per serving than the grocery staples that replace them, their flavors can be unfamiliar to children, and sodium levels vary widely by brand — check labels like any other food.

The grocery pantry's strengths are the mirror image: pennies per serving for rice, oats, beans and pasta; flavors your household already likes; and rotation that refunds its own cost as you eat what you store. Its weaknesses: you must supply the water, fuel and know-how, and shelf lives of everyday packaging are measured in months and years, not decades.

The practical answer for most households is a hybrid: grocery staples as the foundation, plus a small amount of commercial product where it genuinely earns its price — a go-bag meal, an evacuation-ready entrée, a backup for households with no cooking option at all. Never attack-or-buy in panic; test one item of any brand before committing money, and read the nutrition and sodium figures before stocking cases.

The leftover rule for every recipe in this chapter

Without reliable refrigeration, cook and open only what will be eaten this meal. Anything perishable left at room temperature beyond 2 hours (1 hour when it's above 90 °F) belongs in the trash — "when in doubt, throw it out" (USDA). Single-serve packaging is your best tool for enforcing this.

Hot-Water and One-Pot Meals

Forty-eight hot meals built around the two cheapest capabilities in emergency cooking: boiling water and washing one pot.

The Emergency Food Playbook · Food Opsec 19
The Emergency Food PlaybookChapter 6

Hot-water cooking: the fuel thriftiest method

Some foods only need to sit in boiling water — couscous, instant rice, instant potatoes, quick oats, ramen and small pasta in a good thermos. Bring water to a boil once, pour, cover, walk away. Compared with simmering a pot for twenty minutes, this cuts fuel use dramatically, and it works on a single small canister stove or even over boiled water carried in a thermos.

One-pot meals are the next rung: everything happens in a single pot — pasta and sauce, rice and beans, soup from cans. One pot means one cleanup, which means the little water you spend washing dishes stays little.

The four rules of hot-water thrift

  • Cover the pot. A lid shortens boil time and saves fuel on every single meal.
  • Cook short grains first. Instant rice, couscous and quick oats before pasta; pasta before dry beans.
  • Use the package ratio. Absorbed water is free dishwater — drained water is spent twice.
  • Batch only when safe. Cook extra only if leftovers can be eaten within 2 hours, kept above 140 °F, or chilled below 40 °F — otherwise cook per meal.

The flavor system: beating menu fatigue

Menu fatigue — the point where safe food stops being appetizing — is real, and small flavor additions are the cheapest cure in the book. Stock a spice kit: salt, black pepper, bouillon cubes (chicken, beef, vegetable), garlic powder, onion powder, Italian seasoning, chili powder, taco seasoning, curry powder, and at least one hot sauce. Add shelf condiments: soy sauce, salsa, tomato sauce, mustard, relish, honey and jam.

Rule of thumb per 4-serving pot: 1–2 teaspoons dried herbs, 1 bouillon cube, or 1–2 tablespoons of a sauce — taste as you go. Serve spice on the side so adults get heat and children get mild. A drizzle of honey on oats, a spoon of salsa on rice, a splash of hot sauce on chili: these one-cent additions are the difference between tolerating your pantry and enjoying it.

Protein: what most pantries miss

A pantry of rice, pasta, flour and sugar is a calorie pantry, not a food supply — it is short on protein, fat and fiber, and meals built from it fatigue fast. Shelf-stable protein is the highest-value upgrade you can make, and grocery shelves carry more of it than most people realize: canned chicken, tuna, salmon, sardines, ham, chili and beans; dried beans, lentils and split peas; peanut butter and powdered peanut butter; powdered eggs; shelf-stable protein drinks; jerky; textured vegetable protein (TVP); and shelf-stable tofu where available.

The FDA's 50 g Daily Value for protein is a useful yardstick: plan meals that add up to roughly one to two labels' worth per adult per day, and verify with the products you actually buy. Sodium is the trade-off to watch — many canned proteins carry 20–40% of a day's sodium per serving.

Fats: concentrated calories with an expiration date

Fats deliver more than twice the calories per ounce of carbohydrates or protein, and they carry flavor and cooking capability — but they are also the part of the pantry that goes rancid first. Stock peanut butter and other nut or seed butters, cooking oil, coconut oil, shortening, nuts and seeds, shelf-stable cheese products and canned olives, and rotate them by date: most oils keep about a year unopened in cool, dark storage and smell sharp or paint-like when turning. Brown rice and whole-grain flours carry their own oils and keep months, not years. The rule: buy fats in sizes you will rotate within their label life.

Fiber and produce get the same neglect in most lists. Canned vegetables, canned tomatoes, canned fruit, applesauce, fruit cups, dried and freeze-dried fruit, dehydrated vegetables, beans, lentils, oats and whole grains keep variety and fiber in the menu — not as medicine, but as the difference between a diet your family sustains and one they resent. Include at least two vegetable and two fruit items per person per day in your menu math.

Children: familiarity beats novelty every time

Children eat what they recognize. Keep emergency portions of foods they already love — mac and cheese, pancakes, oatmeal, peanut butter crackers, pasta with tomato sauce, mild rice bowls, applesauce, cereal with shelf milk — and build new emergency foods through family taste tests while the lights are on. Serve spice and toppings on the side, keep portions small (seconds are better than waste), and use build-your-own formats so each child controls the outcome. For children with allergies, read every substitute label: seed butters replace peanut butter, oat milk cartons replace dairy, and "made on shared equipment" lines matter for some households.

Remember hydration: children dehydrate faster than adults in heat. Offer water on a schedule, not just on request, and favor salty-snack-and-water pairs over salty snacks alone.

Older adults and access needs

  • Easy-open packaging: prioritize pull-tab cans, pouches and single-serve formats; keep a backup opener and check hand-strength needs honestly.
  • Lower chewing effort: instant mash, congee-style rice porridge, oatmeal, soft canned fruit, soups and smooth peanut butter all rate high.
  • Sodium and medication schedules: plan low-sodium versions of the menu and store food where the routine already lives; medication timing is a household plan — confirm specifics with a clinician or pharmacist.
  • Vision-friendly labels: mark shelves and cans with large-print tape labels (contents and dates).
  • Mobility: store emergency food between knee and shoulder height — not on top shelves, not on the basement stairs.
  • Communication: keep a written list of dietary needs and medications with the emergency kit for responders and relatives.
Infants

Breastfeeding can and should continue in emergencies. For formula-fed infants, CDC guidance is clear: ready-to-feed formula is the safest option (it needs no mixing water); never dilute formula to stretch it; use prepared formula within 2 hours of preparation — within 1 hour once feeding begins — and discard any leftovers from a started bottle. Clean feeding supplies carefully, or use disposable single-serve formats.

Eight habits that rotate a pantry by themselves

  1. New goes behind old. Every purchase slides behind the existing stock, like a grocery shelf.
  2. Mark the dates. Purchase date on arrival, open date on the lid when opened — a paint pen on cans beats sticky notes.
  3. Keep a working pantry. Emergency food lives with everyday food, not in a sealed tomb; you cannot rotate what you never touch.
  4. Eat one emergency meal weekly or monthly (Chapter 7) and score it.
  5. Review inventory monthly against the printed sheet in the back matter.
  6. Inspect quarterly: cans for swelling, rust and deep dents; bags for pests and torn seals; oils by smell.
  7. Replace what was used — the use-one-replace-one rule, same item, same size.
  8. Assign shelves by urgency: shortest shelf life at eye level and in front; long-life staples behind and below.

Where to store — and where not to

Good storage is cool, dark, dry and pest-proof. Interior closets and pantries work; hot garages and attics do not — summer heat can push storage far above room temperature and ages food quickly. Damp basements rust cans and grow mold; if you must store there, keep food off the floor on shelving and watch humidity. Keep food away from gasoline, paints and cleaning chemicals, and never store non-food items on food shelves.

Keep labels intact and readable — an unlabeled can is a guessing game — and prefer packages sized to one meal: a #10 can or a 25-pound bag is only practical if you can use it quickly once opened. Smaller packages rotate faster, store safer and cost a little more; for most households the trade is worth it.

Flood salvage — the narrow exception

Food touched by floodwater is discarded — with one exception USDA describes: undamaged, commercially canned items in waterproof cans. Carbon monoxide warning — read before cooking

Never use generators, charcoal grills, camp stoves, propane heaters or other fuel-burning appliances indoors or in enclosed or partially enclosed spaces — including garages, carports, tents and screened porches — unless specifically approved for that environment and used exactly as directed. Cook outdoors at least 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, with exhaust away from the house. Keep a battery-powered or battery-backup carbon monoxide alarm in the home. CO is colorless and odorless; headache, dizziness, nausea or confusion means fresh air immediately — then 911.

Fuel planning that does not pretend

Exact fuel numbers depend on stove, pot, wind and altitude, so plan in meal-types rather than minutes. As a rough planning anchor (not a guarantee), a standard 16.4 oz propane cylinder on a small camp stove will typically bring roughly 8–12 gallons of water to a boil over its life — enough for a family's hot-water meals for several days if meals are chosen for fuel thrift, or one long weekend of real cooking if they are not. Buy your own fuel, time one boil at home, and do the arithmetic for your own stove — ten minutes of practice replaces guesswork.

Charcoal math: plan roughly 30 briquettes per large-pot meal. Butane canisters boil about 3–5 liters each in typical conditions. In every case, choose meals by fuel category from the recipe spec strips and spend the big fuel on batch days, not daily simmering.

Fuel thrift, in order of impact

  1. Choose short-cook foods: instant rice, instant potatoes, couscous, quick-cooking lentils and quick oats before pasta, and pasta before dry beans.
  2. Soak when safe: an overnight soak in cool, clean conditions cuts bean cook time; in warm conditions without refrigeration, use canned beans instead.
  3. Lids on, always — a covered pot boils faster and holds heat for the steep.
  4. Thermos-finish everything you can: boil 2–5 minutes, then let an insulated vessel finish rice, couscous, oats and soups off the flame.
  5. One pot, one meal, one cleanup; batch cook only when leftovers can be handled safely.
  6. Windbreaks for outdoor stoves — wind can double fuel use.
  7. Skip the boil entirely on no-cook days; hot morale can come from cocoa or broth heated in a cup's worth of water.

Tornado

Playbook
  • Likely challenge: Minutes of warning; possible damage to the house itself, including the pantry; utilities may fail locally.
  • Best food format: A compact go-bin of no-cook food and water that fits in the shelter area itself.
  • Water concern: Small stored amount in the shelter area (1 gallon/person) plus the main supply elsewhere.
  • Cooking concern: Assume nothing until damage is checked; no-cook first, outdoor stove only if the area is safe.
  • Storage concern: Spread supplies across two locations so one destroyed room does not take the whole supply.
  • First actions: Know your shelter spot and put a snack/water bin in it; After: check cans and jars before eating — discard damaged ones; Smell-check for gas before any flame.
  • Avoid: Using flames before checking for gas leaks; Eating from crushed, dented or open cans found in debris.

Wildfire evacuation

Playbook
  • Likely challenge: Leave fast, possibly for days; smoke, heat and ash; shelter or hotel living with no kitchen.
  • Best food format: Evacuation meals: package-eaten, no-cook, heat-tolerant; nothing in glass.
  • Water concern: Bottled water in the car — one gallon per person minimum for the first days.
  • Cooking concern: None while evacuating; a car-bin camp stove only when stopped somewhere safe and legal.
  • Storage concern: What stays home may sit in smoke and ash — keep the deep pantry sealed in bins.

Earthquake

Playbook
  • Likely challenge: No warning; broken glass and open cans in the pantry; gas and water mains may be disrupted.
  • Best food format: Cans and pouches survive shaking; glass jars do not. Favor metal and flexible packaging.
  • Water concern: Water heater and jugs may survive; assume mains are suspect until cleared — treat per Chapter 3.
  • Cooking concern: Check for gas leaks before any flame; then outdoor stove as usual.
  • Storage concern: Secure shelves to walls; heavy cans low; latches on cabinet doors prevent the pantry becoming shrapnel.
  • First actions: After shaking: shoes on, check cans for damage, photograph losses; Shut off gas if you smell it, before cooking anything; Eat undamaged ready foods first.
  • Avoid: Lighting any stove before checking for gas odor; Eating from broken-seal or punctured cans.
  • Stage near the exit: Go-bag by the door; earthquake-prone homes should keep a 72-hour bin in the car too.

Extended power outage

Playbook
  • Likely challenge: Days to weeks without power — the core scenario this book is built for; food safety and morale are the grind.
  • Best food format: Full three-layer system in order: fridge → freezer → Layer 1 → Layer 2 → Layer 3.
  • Water concern: Track consumption daily against the Chapter 3 table; refill from treatment if needed.

Playbook
  • Likely challenge: Hours to overnight stuck or displaced; heat or cold inside the vehicle; no kitchen at all.
  • Best food format: Package-eaten only: bars, trail mix, jerky, pouches, crackers, nut butter packets, single-serve fruit.

About Food Opsec

Food Opsec is a practical-preparedness publisher focused on one idea: households are safest when their emergency plans are built from food they already know, like and can afford. The Food Opsec approach rejects fear-based marketing in favor of checklists, menus, rotation habits and honest math about cost, water and fuel.

The Emergency Food Playbook is the flagship of the Household Resilience Series. It was written against current U.S. federal guidance from FEMA, USDA, FDA, CDC and EPA, and designed to be printed, marked up, taped inside pantry doors, and used until the pages soften.

Preparedness is not a product you buy once. It is a Tuesday-night habit that pays for itself every time the lights go out.

Keep the system alive

Eat one pantry meal this month. Score it. Replace it. That single loop is the entire secret of the book.
